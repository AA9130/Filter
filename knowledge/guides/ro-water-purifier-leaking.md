# Why Is My RO Water Purifier Leaking?

**My RO purifier is leaking — what is causing it and what should I do?**

Almost always at a joint rather than through a component. The usual causes are a push-fit tube that was not seated fully or has been disturbed, a perished O-ring in a filter housing after a cartridge change, a housing overtightened until it deformed, a cracked storage-tank fitting, or a faucet base that has loosened. Do this first: close the feed valve at the mains connection, then the tank valve, then dry everything so you can see where water reappears. Do not keep running the system while you diagnose — an under-sink leak damages the cabinet long before it reaches your floor.

## Key facts

- Close the feed valve first — it stops the damage immediately
- Most leaks are at joints, not through components
- A leak found at the highest wet point is usually the source; water travels down
- A perished O-ring after a cartridge change is the single most common cause

## Do this before anything else

Thirty seconds of the right actions saves the cabinet.

### Close the feed valve

It is the small valve where the system tees off your cold supply, usually at the back of the cupboard. Turning it off stops water entering the system at all.

### Close the tank valve

On a tanked system, the valve on top of the storage tank. This stops stored water continuing to feed the leak.

### Dry everything and put paper down

You cannot find a leak in a wet cupboard. Dry it, lay kitchen paper under the unit, and you will see exactly where water reappears and in what order.

### Do not run it to 'check'

The instinct to turn it back on and watch is understandable and expensive. Diagnose dry.

## The usual suspects, in order of how often we find them

### A perished housing O-ring

The most common cause, and it usually appears shortly after a cartridge change. The O-ring is compressed, ages in heat, and does not re-seat reliably once disturbed. New O-rings should be standard at every cartridge change, not an optional extra.

### An overtightened housing

If a housing needed force to stop weeping, the O-ring was already the problem — and the force may have deformed the bowl or damaged the thread. Once deformed, no amount of tightening seals it.

### A push-fit tube not fully seated

These fittings grip and seal in one motion, and a tube pushed in only most of the way will hold for weeks before letting go. Also common after someone has moved things around under the sink.

### A tube cut at an angle

An angled cut cannot seal squarely against the fitting's O-ring. This is why a leaking push-fit joint is cut back and re-cut square rather than simply pushed in harder.

### A cracked storage-tank fitting

The plastic port on the tank is a stress point, particularly if the tank has been knocked or the tube has been pulled. Usually shows as a slow weep at the top of the tank.

### A loose faucet base

The drinking faucet seals against the sink or worktop from below. It loosens over time with use and shows up as water appearing around the tap, or inside the cupboard directly beneath it.

### A weeping drain saddle

The connection to the sink waste. The least obvious leak, because it drips into a cabinet floor that people rarely look at, and the one most often found by smell rather than sight.

## How to trace it properly

Water travels down and sideways, so the wet patch is rarely the source. Work from the top of the system downwards, and look for the highest wet point rather than the largest one.

With the cupboard dry, open the feed valve alone and wait. If water appears before the tank valve is opened, the leak is on the pressurised feed side — the housings, the pre-filters, the membrane housing. If it only appears once the tank valve is open, look at the tank fitting, the post-filter and the faucet line.

## What you can reasonably do yourself, and what you should not

### Reasonable

Closing the valves. Drying and observing. Hand-tightening a faucet base nut. Reseating a push-fit tube after cutting it back square with a proper tube cutter.

### Not reasonable

Forcing a housing tighter. Reusing an O-ring that has already failed. Sealing a joint with tape or compound — push-fit and O-ring joints are engineered to seal without either, and adding them usually makes the leak worse. And leaving a system running with a known leak because it is 'only a drip'.

## Why this happens more in the UAE

Heat. Plastic housings, O-rings and push-fit tubing all age faster at higher ambient temperatures, and an under-sink cabinet next to an oven, or a plant room with no shade, runs hotter than the numbers on the datasheet assume. Components that would give five years in a temperate climate can perish sooner here.

That is a maintenance implication rather than a reason for alarm: O-rings and tubing are cheap, they are accessible at every service visit, and replacing them proactively is far cheaper than replacing a kitchen cabinet.
