# Tank vs Tankless RO Purifier: Which Suits Your Kitchen?

**Should I buy a tanked or a tankless RO water purifier?**

Neither is better — they trade different things. A tanked system stores several litres of purified water, so it delivers a fast flow from a modest pump, but the tank takes cupboard space and water sits in storage between draws. A tankless system purifies as you draw, so nothing is stored and it needs far less depth, but it requires a higher-output membrane and a pump to deliver a usable flow rate, which raises the purchase price. Tight apartment cupboard: tankless. Large family drawing several litres at once: tanked.

## Key facts

- Tanked delivers faster flow because the water is already purified and stored
- Tankless takes much less cupboard depth because there is no storage vessel
- Tankless costs more for the same filtration — you pay for the space saving and on-demand behaviour
- A tank's air charge is a maintenance item; losing it presents as low pressure at the faucet

## Why the difference exists at all

An RO membrane produces water slowly. That is inherent — it is a pressure-driven separation, not a filter that water simply flows through. A residential membrane cannot fill a jug at the rate a tap can.

There are only two ways around that. Store purified water ahead of demand, which is what a tank does. Or make the membrane and pump big enough to keep up with demand as it happens, which is what a tankless system does. Everything else about the comparison follows from that single engineering choice.

## The comparison

_Tanked and tankless RO compared_

| | Tanked RO | Tankless RO |

| --- | --- | --- |

| **Flow at the faucet** | Fast — drawn from storage | Slower — limited by production rate |

| **Cupboard space** | Unit plus a separate tank | Single slim unit |

| **Water stored between draws** | Yes, several litres | None |

| **Purchase price for equivalent filtration** | Lower | Higher |

| **Pump requirement** | Modest, or none with good pressure | Higher output, usually essential |

| **Extra maintenance item** | Tank air charge | None equivalent |

| **Sensitivity to low feed pressure** | Buffered by the tank | Felt immediately |

| **Best suited to** | Families, larger draws, adequate space | Tight cupboards, smaller households, countertop use |

## How to decide in about two minutes

### Measure the cupboard, including depth behind the waste trap

This is the decisive measurement and the one people skip. Behind the trap is where the space actually runs out. If a unit plus a separate tank will not fit, the decision is made for you.

### Count how much water you draw at once

Filling a two-litre jug is easy for either. Filling a pot for cooking plus several bottles, back to back, is where on-demand production starts to feel slow. A larger household is the case for a tank.

### Check your feed pressure

Both types are hurt by low pressure, but tankless is hurt more, because it has no reservoir to draw on while the membrane catches up. On a high floor, confirm pressure before choosing tankless.

### Decide how you feel about stored water

Water in a tank stands between draws. It is not unsafe, but taste flattens if the household draws little, and the first glass after a few days is noticeably different. Some people care about this; most do not once it is explained.

## The maintenance difference nobody mentions at the point of sale

A tanked system has one extra failure mode: the tank's air charge. A pressure tank holds purified water against a diaphragm with a charge of air behind it. Lose the charge and the tank still fills with water but cannot push it out, which presents at the faucet as low pressure — a symptom that people almost always attribute to the membrane.

It is quick to diagnose and quick to correct, and it is the single most common reason we are called out to a tanked system for 'low pressure'. A tankless system does not have this failure mode, which is a genuine, if unglamorous, advantage.

## The short version

Measure the cupboard first and count your peak draw second. If both fit a tanked system, it is the cheaper and better-buffered choice. If the cupboard is tight, tankless exists precisely for that, and the price difference buys space rather than better water.
